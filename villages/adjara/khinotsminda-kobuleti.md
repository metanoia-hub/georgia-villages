## History
- Khinotsminda (khinotsminda, Хиноцминда) was officially called Khino (khino, Хино) before Government Decree No. 46 of 2 February 2018 renamed it. ([source](https://www.matsne.gov.ge/ka/document/view/4044783))
- Iuri Sikharulidze’s 1959 field study identifies Zemo Khino (Upper Khino) as the village with the cathedral ruins. It identifies Kvemo Khino (Lower Khino) as Didvake. The same study records the Khino name in Vakhushti Bagrationi’s 18th-century geography and the Khinotsminda form in Gurian princely documents. ([source](https://iverieli.nplg.gov.ge/bitstream/1234/496770/1/Samxret-Dasavlet_Saqartvelos_Toponimika_1959_N2.pdf))
- The medieval site was an episcopal centre. Georgia Travel dates the end of the episcopal see to the second half of the 18th century under Ottoman rule and names a local elder, Tsetskhladze, as a later caretaker. ([source](https://georgia.travel/khino-monastery-complex))
- Sikharulidze quotes Zakaria Chichinadze’s account that 40 households left Zemo Khino as muhajirs in the period after the 1877–78 Russo-Ottoman War. ([source](https://iverieli.nplg.gov.ge/bitstream/1234/496770/1/Samxret-Dasavlet_Saqartvelos_Toponimika_1959_N2.pdf))
- The 1923 roll for Zemo Khino records families including Tsetskhladze (tsetskhladze), Kartsivadze (kartsivadze), Katamadze (katamadze), Beridze (beridze) and Madoishvili (madoishvili). ([source](https://journal.archives-ajara.gov.ge/index.php/home/article/download/108/87/235#page=2))
- All 94 residents recorded in the 2002 census under the name Khino were recorded as ethnic Georgian. ([source](https://geostat.ge/media/44560/II-tomi-.pdf#page=32))
- In a 2017 Ekofact interview at the monastery, Abbot Evdemoz said he had lived there alone for two years. The article also interviews people in a lower settlement it calls Khino, separately from the monastery visit farther uphill. ([source](https://ekofact.com/2017/11/18/%E1%83%99%E1%83%98%E1%83%9C%E1%83%A2%E1%83%A0%E1%83%98%E1%83%A8%E1%83%98%E1%83%A1-%E1%83%93%E1%83%90%E1%83%AA%E1%83%A3%E1%83%9A%E1%83%98-%E1%83%A2%E1%83%94%E1%83%A0%E1%83%98%E1%83%A2%E1%83%9D%E1%83%A0/))
- The Kintrishi protected-area management plan describes three people wintering at the Khino monastery and seasonal returns to cultivate land. ([source](https://matsne.gov.ge/ka/document/view/5161488?publication=2))
- Founding date, documented causes of the late-20th-century decline, and identity of the 2024 census resident — nothing found.

## Crafts And Traditions
- Laz stonemasons were working in Khino in 1919, according to village oral testimony recorded in 1957 and published by Iuri Sikharulidze. ([source](https://iverieli.nplg.gov.ge/bitstream/1234/496770/1/Samxret-Dasavlet_Saqartvelos_Toponimika_1959_N2.pdf))
- The 1923 Zemo Khino household roll records cultivated land and livestock including horses, cattle, sheep and goats. ([source](https://journal.archives-ajara.gov.ge/index.php/home/article/download/108/87/235#page=3))
- The protected-area management plan records 15 agricultural plots in Khino and a guesthouse operating in 2017 and 2018. It describes beekeeping as a tradition of the wider Kintrishi Valley. ([source](https://matsne.gov.ge/ka/document/view/5161488?publication=2))
- Khinotsmindoba is held on 11 September, the Orthodox commemoration of the Beheading of John the Baptist. It includes a liturgy and a public celebration with Adjarian folklore, traditional crafts and local products. ([source](https://mzk.ge/ge/istorija-i-kulytrua/90701-khonotsmindoba-dghesastsauli-mtian-acharashi))

## Churches, Towers, Monuments
- Khino monastic complex, including the medieval church ruins, enclosure wall and other structures. The national heritage register lists it at Khino as monument 3453, entered on 3 October 2007. ([source](https://www.heritagesites.ge/uploads/files/6989e0438f7c0.pdf#page=111))
- The old cathedral was dedicated to John the Baptist. Georgia Travel places it in the 9th–10th centuries on architectural grounds. ([source](https://georgia.travel/khino-monastery-complex))
- Kobuleti municipality’s guide dates the old cathedral to the 10th–11th centuries. ([source](https://kobuleti.gov.ge/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/gidi-1.pdf#page=19))
- Sikharulidze records evidence of damage before Dimitri Bakradze’s 1873 visit and oral testimony about the demolition of surviving cathedral walls in 1919. ([source](https://iverieli.nplg.gov.ge/bitstream/1234/496770/1/Samxret-Dasavlet_Saqartvelos_Toponimika_1959_N2.pdf))
- A modern wooden church dedicated to the Nativity of John the Baptist was built beside the old ruins, and a men’s monastery was founded there. ([source](https://mzk.ge/ge/istorija-i-kulytrua/90701-khonotsmindoba-dghesastsauli-mtian-acharashi))
